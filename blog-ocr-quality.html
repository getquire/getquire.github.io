OCR does not fail loudly, and that is the problem

A recogniser that returns garbage is easy to handle. A recogniser that returns a plausible wrong digit with 94% confidence is how a scanned invoice becomes a different invoice.

TWTal Weiss · Maintainer, ingestion
11 August 2026

Roughly 11% of the documents our users index have no text layer at all, and another 6% have one that is worse than nothing. For those, recognition is the whole pipeline: whatever OCR produces is the document, and every later stage inherits its mistakes without being able to see them.

Modern recognisers are good. Character error rates on clean office scans sit under half a percent, which sounds like a solved problem and is not, for two reasons: errors are not uniformly distributed, and confidence scores are not calibrated.

Prose is protected, numbers are not

An OCR error in a sentence is usually survivable. Context does the repair — a reader, a retriever and a language model all reconstruct "recieve" or "1nvoice" without effort, because the surrounding words constrain what the token could have been.

Digits have no such context. In an amount field, any digit is as plausible as any other. There is nothing in the string that says 7 is unlikely here. So the exact same recogniser, at the exact same quality, has a benign error rate in prose and a dangerous one in a number.

In prose an OCR error is a typo. In an amount field it is a different invoice, and nothing downstream will notice.

Our measurements on the scanned subset of the benchmark corpus, per field type:

Field typeCharacter error rateField-level error rateRecoverable by context
Body prose0.4%n/aAlmost always
Vendor name0.6%2.1%Usually, against a known vendor list
Dates0.9%3.4%Partly, format constraints help
Invoice number1.3%6.8%Rarely, they are arbitrary strings
Amounts1.1%5.2%Never, without arithmetic
Amounts, low-quality scan3.7%16.9%Never, without arithmetic

A 5.2% field-level error rate on amounts means one invoice in twenty has a wrong number in it, presented with no visible sign that anything went wrong. That is the number that should decide your architecture.

Confidence scores are a genre of fiction

Every recogniser will tell you how sure it is. We plotted reported confidence against measured accuracy across 300,000 recognised numeric fields, and the relationship is real but weak: within the 90-to-99 band, which is where most output lands, reported confidence carries almost no information about whether the field is right.

The systematic confusions are the familiar ones — 0 and 8, 1 and 7, 3 and 8, 5 and 6, and on European handwriting the 1 that has a serif long enough to be a 7. In every case the recogniser is confident, because the glyph genuinely does look like the thing it decided on.

What we do instead

  • Two engines must agree on any digit run. Character-level agreement between independent recognisers is a far better calibrated signal than either one's confidence. Disagreement on a numeric span marks it unreadable.
  • Arithmetic is the third vote. On invoices the reconciliation check catches most single-digit errors, because a wrong digit almost never leaves the totals consistent. This is covered in the line-item post, and it is the strongest signal we have on any scanned financial document.
  • Unreadable means unreadable. A span that fails both checks is indexed as an image region with no text value. A question that depends on it gets "the scan is not legible here", plus the cropped region so a person can read it themselves in two seconds.
  • Preprocessing is per-page, not per-document. Deskew, denoise and binarisation thresholds get chosen per page from the page itself. Documents are frequently a mix of a clean print and a fax of a fax.
  • Never a language model as a repairer. Asking a model to clean up OCR output produces text that reads beautifully and has quietly invented plausible numbers. We tried it. It improved every readability metric and made the system materially less trustworthy.

The cost, stated honestly

Running two recognisers roughly doubles ingestion CPU time on the scanned subset. On a 40,000-document corpus with 11% scans, that is about 25 minutes of extra work on a laptop-class machine, once, at index time. Nobody who has watched a wrong amount get quoted back to them with full confidence has argued about the 25 minutes.

Refusing on illegible spans also lowers coverage: about 1.4% of questions against heavily scanned corpora return "not legible" instead of an answer. We consider that the correct number to be non-zero. A system that answers every question about a smudged fax is not better at reading, it is better at guessing.

Engine selection is configurable in quire.toml; the default pair is chosen for licence compatibility rather than benchmark scores, and both run fully offline.
