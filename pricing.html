Why it's free

There is no paid tier.
Here's how that holds.

"Free" is a promise about the future, and most projects that make it have a business model quietly working against it. This page is the arithmetic — what it costs to run, where the money comes from, and what happens when that stops.

Apache-2.0, irrevocably

Every release is licensed at publication. A licence already granted can't be withdrawn, so every version you've installed stays free regardless of anything we decide later.

No contributor licence agreement

We deliberately don't collect the rights that would let us relicense. About 40% of the codebase is contributed, and we couldn't make it proprietary without 340 people's permission.

No investors

Nobody holds equity that needs a return. The pressure that turns open projects into open cores doesn't exist here, because the cap table doesn't exist here.

What sponsorship does and doesn't buy

Money doesn't buy merges.

Sponsors get

  • A logo on the README and this site, if they want one
  • A monthly written update on where the money went
  • Priority on triage — their issues get read first
  • An hour a month with a maintainer, if they use it

Sponsors do not get

  • Features merged because they asked and paid
  • A private fork, an early build, or an exclusive anything
  • A vote in governance or a seat on the maintainer team
  • Any influence over the roadmap that a non-sponsor lacks

Support retainers, stated plainly

Three organizations pay for guaranteed response time and help with their own deployments. This is the closest thing to commerce here, and it's worth naming the risk: paid support can create a quiet incentive to leave the software confusing. Our check on that is crude but real — every support question that took more than one exchange becomes a documentation issue, and we publish the count each quarter.
